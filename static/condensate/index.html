Implementation notes

Each model evolves a quasi-2D Gross–Pitaevskii equation, iψt = [−½∇² + V + Vbeam + g|ψ|² + …]ψ, in trap units (ħ = m = ω = 1). The equation is solved by split-step Fourier on a 128 × 128 grid, with weak damping so stirred clouds slowly settle.

Dipolar: dipoles tilted by α from the normal, with their in-plane part at azimuth φ from x, and the quasi-2D kernel gεdd[cos²α (2 − 3F) + sin²α (−1 + 3F k∥²/k²)], k∥ = kx cos φ + ky sin φ, F = F(klz/√2) = √π q eq²erfc q (Fischer 2006; Ticknor, Wilson and Bohn 2011), which reduces to the out-of-plane kernel at α = 0 and to gεdd(3cos²α − 1) at long wavelengths. A Lee–Huang–Yang term γ(1 + 3εdd²/2)|ψ|³ stops collapse. Tilted dipoles make the kernel strongly repulsive at short wavelengths along x, which the split-step scheme only tolerates with a smaller step, so the time step is 0.008/(1 + 0.8 εdd sin²α).

Standard: the plain GPE, with the time derivative multiplied by (1 − iγ). The split-step method is unitary, so γ = 0 runs indefinitely and conserves energy. The only dissipation left is a spectral filter on the top fifth of wavenumbers, which keeps energy from piling up at the grid scale.

Vortex taps: in “Add vortices” mode a tap imprints a quantised vortex, ψ → ψ · r/√(r² + 2ξ²) · e±iθ, with ξ taken from the local density. In a mixture it goes into both species, or only the red one with “Act on red only”. Markers come from the phase winding around each grid plaquette, wherever the smoothed density is above a fifth of its peak; red is +1 (clockwise on screen) and blue is −1, following the red species in a mixture. With no trap the periodic box can't hold a net circulation, so vortex taps are off there.

Rabi / spin–orbit: a mixture with Raman coupling (Ω/2)σx and spin–orbit coupling of the kind used at NIST, H = ½(px − ksoσz)² + ½py² + (Ω/2)σx, with g11 = g22 = g. The spin–orbit term only shifts each species' kinetic energy to ½k² ∓ ksokx, and the coupling is an exact local rotation, so both stay exact in the split-step scheme. Atoms move between species, so the total is normalised and μ is shared. The lower band has minima at ±k1 = ±kso√(1 − (Ω/2kso²)²) while Ω < 2kso²: the condensate fills both (stripes, with each species at opposite momenta) when g12 < g, and one (a plane wave) when g12 > g; above 2kso² it sits at zero momentum. Cooling starts from the lower-band spinors at ±k1 so it can choose. With kso = 0 it is a Rabi-coupled mixture. Show phase displays the relative phase of the species in both mixture models.

Two-component: two species with equal self-interaction g and cross-interaction g12. They mix when g12 < g and separate when g12 > g. Each species also feels the Petrov Lee–Huang–Yang term γm(n1 + n2)3/2, which matters only near g12 = −g. Below that, mean-field attraction would collapse the mixture, and quantum fluctuations instead hold it as a self-bound droplet at density n0 = (5δ/12γm)², with δ = −(g + g12). With no trap the box is periodic, so atoms that leave a droplet come back round as a dilute gas.

Self-gravitating: Newtonian gravity, averaged over the same thin axial profile and solved by FFT (Schrödinger–Poisson). The simulation box is periodic, so the uniform background is removed and image clouds add a small tidal error.

Polariton: the driven-dissipative GPE with an incoherent reservoir nR (Wouters–Carusotto): iψt = [−½∇² + V + g|ψ|² + gRnR + (i/2)(RnR − γc)]ψ, with ∂tnR = P − (γR + R|ψ|²)nR. Density is set by gain against loss, not fixed, and threshold is at Pth = γcγR/R. The trap buttons also shape the pump: a Gaussian spot, a top hat, an annulus or uniform. Weak relaxation on the kinetic term damps the shortest waves. The noise is the truncated-Wigner term from gain and loss (Wouters–Savona), with diffusion (γc + RnR)/2 ≈ γc at clamping, scaled by σ and projected onto |k| < 6.5. Disorder is a Gaussian-correlated random potential, ⟨V(0)V(r)⟩ ∝ e−r²/ℓ² with ℓ = 0.6, of the chosen rms height. As in the thermal model, the density shown is averaged over 0.25 trap units, while the phase view is instantaneous.

Rotating: the contact GPE in the frame rotating at Ω, with H − ΩLz. Because Lz is diagonal in neither real nor Fourier space, the kinetic and rotation terms are split by alternating directions (−½∂x² + Ωykx, then −½∂y² − Ωxky). Cooling starts from roughly the Feynman number of vortices, Ω/π per unit area, so the lattice forms quickly. With no trap the rotating frame is unbounded, so that option is off.

Thermal: the simple-growth stochastic projected GPE, dψ = 𝒫{−(i + γ)(L − μ)ψ dt + dW} with ⟨dW*dW⟩ = 2γT dt. A reservoir at temperature T and chemical potential μ = 12 exchanges atoms with the field, so atom number fluctuates. The projector keeps |k| < 6.5. The density shown is a running average over 0.25 trap units, as an absorption image would blur it; the phase view is instantaneous. The temperature where vortex pairs unbind depends on the cutoff, as usual for classical-field methods.

Grid size: where the browser supports WebGPU, the whole solver runs on the graphics processor. The grid spacing stays fixed, so a bigger grid is a bigger system rather than a finer one: on a grid s times wider, the harmonic trap frequency is 1/s, the box and ring radii are s times larger and the atom number s² times larger, which keeps the density, healing length and chemical potential the same. Local features keep their physical size: the box wall, the lattice period, the disorder length and the ring's cross-section. The ring therefore only grows in circumference, so its atom number scales as s. Rotation and gravity are scaled by 1/s for the same reason. Without WebGPU, the simulation runs on the processor at 128².

Traps are circular: a harmonic ½r², a box with a smooth wall, a ring ½ωr²(r − R)², a square optical lattice VL[sin²(πx/a) + sin²(πy/a)] (depth in recoil energies ER = π²/2a²) on top of the harmonic trap, or none.

Foundations: the Gross–Pitaevskii equation and how to read the picture

The equation

A dilute Bose gas well below its condensation temperature is described by a single complex field, the condensate wavefunction ψ(𝐫,t), normalised to the atom number, ∫|ψ|2d2r=N. It obeys the Gross–Pitaevskii equation (BBP §3.1)

iℏ∂ψ∂t=[−ℏ22m∇2+V(𝐫)+g|ψ|2]ψ.

The nonlinear term is the mean field of s-wave contact collisions. The app is two-dimensional: the gas is assumed to be frozen in a narrow Gaussian profile along z, of width lz, so the 3D coupling is reduced to g2D=g3D/(2πlz) (BBP §3.5.4). Everything is in trap units (BBP §3.8.2): ℏ=m=1, times in 1/ω and lengths in aho=ℏ/mω, where ω is the harmonic trap frequency. Here g=1, N=475 and lz=1.5 at 128².

Reading the picture

Brightness is the density n=|ψ|2. “Show phase” shows the phase θ of ψ=neiθ as colour. The Madelung transformation turns the GPE into the equations of an inviscid, irrotational fluid with velocity 𝐯=(ℏ/m)∇θ (BBP §3.3):

∂n∂t+∇·(n𝐯)=0,m∂𝐯∂t+∇(mv22+V+gn−ℏ22m∇2nn)=0.

The last term, the quantum pressure, matters only where the density changes over short distances, such as at the cloud's edge and in vortex cores. Where the phase colours wind once round a point, the density there must vanish: that point is a quantised vortex.

Scales to know

In the Thomas–Fermi limit the kinetic term is negligible in the bulk and n=(μ−V)/g (BBP §3.5.2). For the 2D harmonic trap this gives μ=ℏωgNm/πℏ2, which is 475/π≈12.3 in trap units, and a cloud radius R=2μ/ω≈5.0. The simulated peak density is 12.8, slightly higher because of the kinetic energy at the edge. Three scales follow from the peak density n0:

  • the healing length ξ=ℏ/mgn0≈0.28, the shortest distance over which the density can change and the size of a vortex core (BBP §3.4.2, §7.4);
  • the speed of sound c=gn0/m≈3.6 (BBP §4.1.2);
  • the Bogoliubov dispersion ϵ(k)=ℏ2k22m(ℏ2k22m+2gn0), linear (phonons) for kξ≪1 and free-particle-like for kξ≫1 (BBP §4.1.1).

The visible field is 13.3 trap units across for the harmonic, box and lattice traps, 13.6 for the ring and 17 with no trap. On larger grids the field and the cloud grow by s=N/128 while ξ and c stay fixed.

Quantised vortices

Because ψ is single-valued, the phase can only change by a multiple of 2π around a closed loop, so the circulation is quantised (BBP §7.1–7.3):

∮𝐯·dℓ=qhm,vθ=qℏmr,q=±1,±2,…

The core has radius of order ξ, and the energy of a vortex grows as ln(R/ξ) with the system size (BBP §7.4–7.5). In 2D, vortices move like point vortices: each is carried by the flow of all the others (BBP §8.2, §9.1).

How it is computed

The app uses the split-step Fourier method: half a step of the potential and nonlinear terms in real space, a full kinetic step in Fourier space, then the second half-step (BBP Appendix A.1–A.2; Bao, Jaksch and Markowich 2003). Ground states come from imaginary-time evolution, t→−iτ, which damps every excited state faster than the ground state (BBP Appendix A.3). Real-time runs can include phenomenological damping, replacing i∂t by (i−γ)∂t, which models energy loss to a thermal cloud (BBP §3.9.1; Choi, Morgan and Burnett 1998). Implementation details are under “Implementation notes” beside the controls.

Traps

Harmonic: V=12(ωx2x2+ωy2y2), with ωx=ω and the aspect slider setting λ=ωy/ωx. In two dimensions the Thomas–Fermi atom number is N=πμ2/(gωxωy), so the app takes N=475/λ: μ, the central density and ξ then match the round trap, and the radii are Ri=2μ/ωi. As Ry falls towards a few ξ the cloud leaves the Thomas–Fermi regime and approaches a quasi-one-dimensional gas (BBP §3.5.4). Box: a flat floor with a smooth wall, as in uniform-gas experiments (Gaunt et al. 2013). Ring: V=12ωr2(r−R)2, the geometry of atomtronic persistent-current experiments (Ramanathan et al. 2011). Lattice: VL[sin2(πx/a)+sin2(πy/a)] on a harmonic trap, with depth in recoil energies (BBP §3.9.3; Morsch and Oberthaler 2006). None: a periodic box, which approximates an infinite uniform gas.

A single condensate with contact interactions: the simplest superfluid. Moved slowly, an obstacle passes through it without drag; moved fast enough, it sheds quantised vortices.

The model

(i−γ)∂ψ∂t=[−12∇2+V+Vbeam+g|ψ|2−μ]ψ,Vbeam=V0e−|𝐫−𝐫b(t)|2/σ2.

The beam is a repulsive Gaussian, like a blue-detuned laser, with V0=60, well above μ, so it bores a hole in the fluid. The slider sets the damping γ; at γ = 0 the dynamics conserve energy.

The physics

Landau's argument says a fluid with excitation spectrum ε(p) can flow past an obstacle without creating excitations below vc=minpϵ(p)/p, which for the Bogoliubov spectrum is the speed of sound c (BBP §6.9). In practice dissipation starts earlier. The flow speeds up around the obstacle, to twice the free-stream speed at its poles for a cylinder in potential flow, and vortex–antivortex pairs nucleate where the local flow becomes supersonic (BBP §9.5). Simulations give a critical velocity of roughly 0.4–0.5c for a large impenetrable obstacle, lower in a trap where the density, and so c, falls towards the edge (Frisch, Pomeau and Rica 1992). Experiments see the same onset (Raman et al. 1999; Neely et al. 2010).

Once shed, the vortices move with the flow. An opposite-sign pair separated by d travels perpendicular to the line joining them at speed ℏ/(md); a same-sign pair orbits its midpoint at angular velocity 2ℏ/(md2) (BBP §8.2, §9.2). In a harmonic trap a single vortex precesses round the centre, and with damping it drifts outwards to lower its energy until it leaves the cloud (Fetter 2009).

Try this

  1. Find the critical velocity. Drag the beam slowly through the cloud: the fluid parts and closes with no wake. Repeat faster. Above a threshold, vortex pairs peel off the beam's sides. The central sound speed here is about 3.6 trap units per unit time, so a sweep across the cloud (10 units) in about 6 time units is near the threshold. Mark vortices to count them.

  2. A vortex dipole. In the box trap, tap a +1 vortex and a −1 vortex about 2 units apart. Predict the direction and speed (about 1/d≈0.5) before you watch it go, then try a same-sign pair and watch it orbit.

  3. Dissipation. Tap a single vortex off-centre in the harmonic trap with γ = 0: it precesses indefinitely. Raise γ to 0.03: it spirals out of the cloud, because a vortex nearer the edge, where the density is lower, has less energy.

  4. A cigar-shaped condensate. Set the trap aspect to λ=ωy/ωx=2, then 6. Thomas–Fermi theory predicts the radii Rx=2μ/ωx≈5.0 and Ry=Rx/λ. At λ = 2 the simulated width ratio is 1.94; at λ = 6 it is only 5.0. Why does the Thomas–Fermi prediction fail as the cloud narrows? Compare Ry with ξ ≈ 0.28. Then drag the beam along the cigar.

A condensate cannot rotate like a solid body, because its flow is irrotational. It rotates by admitting quantised vortices, which arrange themselves into a triangular lattice.

The model

i∂ψ∂t=[−12∇2+V+g|ψ|2−ΩLz]ψ,Lz=−i(x∂∂y−y∂∂x),

written in the frame rotating with the trap at angular velocity Ω (the slider gives Ω/ω).

The physics

In the rotating frame the system minimises E−ΩLz, so a vortex lowers the free energy once Ω exceeds a critical value Ωc1, of order (ℏ/mR2)ln(R/ξ) (BBP §7.6–7.7). That is a few tenths of ω here. The app seeds vortices before cooling, so at small Ω a single vortex may survive as a metastable state below Ωc1.

At higher Ω, many vortices mimic solid-body rotation on average. The coarse-grained vorticity must equal 2Ω, which fixes the areal density of vortices (Feynman 1955; BBP §7.6):

nv=2mΩh=Ωπ (trap units),b=(2π3Ω)1/2

where b is the spacing of the triangular (Abrikosov) lattice that minimises the interaction energy. Centrifugal force weakens the trap to ωeff=ω2−Ω2, so the cloud swells as Ω approaches ω and deconfines at Ω = ω (Fetter 2009). Feynman's rule fixes the density, not the total: near the edge of a finite cloud there is a vortex-free band, so the number of vortices is well below nvπR2.

Try this

  1. Test Feynman's rule. At Ω = 0.7, mark the vortices. Predict the lattice spacing, b≈2.3, about a sixth of the 13.3-unit field. Then count the vortices inside a circle through the outermost ones, of radius Rv, and compare with ΩRv2. The simulation gives about 11 inside a radius of 4.2, against a prediction of 12.

  2. The vortex-free edge. With Thomas–Fermi radius R=(2μ/ωeff2)1/2 and μ=ωeffgN/π, the naive count ΩR2 at Ω = 0.7 is about 24, twice what you see. Where is the missing rotation carried?

  3. Towards deconfinement. Raise Ω to 0.85 and watch the cloud spread and the lattice fill it, as ωeff falls to about 0.53ω.

Atoms with large magnetic moments, such as chromium, erbium and dysprosium, interact through a long-range, anisotropic dipole–dipole force as well as contact collisions. The competition produces a roton, quantum droplets and supersolids.

The model

Two dipoles aligned along 𝐞^ interact through (BBP §12.2)

Udd(𝐫)=Cdd4π1−3cos2ϑr3,εdd=Cdd3g,

attractive head-to-tail and repulsive side by side. Averaging over a Gaussian axial profile gives the quasi-2D interaction in Fourier space, for dipoles tilted by α from the z axis, with their in-plane projection along 𝐞^∥=(cosϕ,sinϕ) (Fischer 2006; Ticknor, Wilson and Bohn 2011; BBP §12.4.2):

U~(𝐤)=g+gεdd[cos2α(2−3F)+sin2α(3Fk∥2k2−1)],k∥=kxcosϕ+kysinϕ, F=πqeq2erfc(q),q=klz2.

A Lee–Huang–Yang (LHY) term γLHY(1+32εdd2)|ψ|3, from quantum fluctuations, stops collapse at high density (Schützhold et al. 2006; Lima and Pelster 2011; BBP §13.1). The app uses the 3D form of this term with γLHY=0.02.

The physics

At α = 0 the interaction is repulsive at long wavelengths, U~→g(1+2εdd), but tends to g(1−εdd) at short wavelengths, where the head-to-tail attraction across the thin axial direction wins. The Bogoliubov spectrum ϵ(k)2=k22(k22+2nU~(k)) therefore develops a roton minimum at k~1/lz as εdd grows (Santos, Shlyapnikov and Lewenstein 2003; BBP §12.7.2). When the roton softens to zero the uniform state gives way to density modulation. The LHY term then stabilises an array of droplets, which, when the droplets stay phase-coherent, is a supersolid (BBP §13.2.2, §13.4; Kadau et al. 2016; Chomaz et al. 2023).

Tilting the dipoles makes the long-wavelength term gεdd(3cos2α−1), which vanishes at the magic angle αm=arccos(1/3)≈54.7∘ and is attractive beyond it. The same factor governs dipoles in a rotating field (BBP §12.6; Giovanazzi, Görlitz and Pfau 2002). An in-plane component also makes the interaction anisotropic, so the cloud stretches along the dipoles' in-plane direction φ: magnetostriction (BBP §12.8.3).

Try this

  1. Magnetostriction. At εdd=0.5, move the tilt from 0° to 90°. The cloud elongates along x; the simulated aspect ratio ⟨x2⟩/⟨y2⟩ goes from 1.0 to about 1.5. Now turn the azimuth φ: the elongation follows the dipoles round, reaching y at φ = 90°.

  2. The magic angle. Compare α = 0 with α = 54.7°. At α = 0 the dipoles add repulsion, so the cloud is larger: its mean density ∫n²/∫n falls from 8.0 without dipoles to 7.5. At the magic angle the isotropic part of the interaction cancels and the mean density returns to 8.0, yet the cloud is elongated, with aspect ratio about 1.3, because the anisotropic part of the kernel survives at finite k.

  3. The roton and droplets. At α = 0, raise εdd slowly towards 2.4. Near 1.9 a ripple appears with a wavelength of a few lz, the roton; near 2.4 the cloud breaks into droplets. Why does the roton wavelength scale with lz rather than with ξ?

  4. Aligned droplets. At large tilt and εdd≈2, the gas forms filaments and droplets lined up along x, the head-to-tail direction. This is slow, because strong tilts need a smaller time step.

  5. Dipoles in a cigar. In a cigar trap (aspect 3) with in-plane dipoles (α = 90°, εdd=1), compare φ = 0, along the cigar, with φ = 90°, across it. Along the cigar the cloud becomes thinner and denser than without dipoles (rms width 0.72 → 0.52, mean density 7.8 → 10.5); across it, wider and more dilute (0.92 and 6.3). Both have the same long-wavelength interaction, so why the difference? Think about which wavenumbers the narrow direction contains, and what U~ is at large k parallel and perpendicular to the dipoles.

Two condensates, for example two hyperfine states of one atom, that interact with themselves and with each other. Depending on the cross-interaction they mix, separate, or bind into a self-supporting quantum droplet.

The model

i∂ψj∂t=[−12∇2+V+g|ψj|2+g12|ψ3−j|2+γm(|ψ1|2+|ψ2|2)3/2]ψj,j=1,2,

with equal self-interactions g11=g22=g and an LHY term with γm=0.04 (BBP §11.1.1, §13.2.1).

The physics

A uniform mixture is miscible when g122<g11g22 and separates otherwise (BBP §11.2; Ao and Chui 1998). Here that is g12/g=1. The Bogoliubov spectrum has a density branch and a spin branch, with the spin branch's speed of sound proportional to (g−g12)n. Beyond the threshold the spin mode is unstable and domains grow and coarsen (BBP §11.7.2). Near the threshold the domain walls are wide, because the spin healing length diverges.

For g12<−g mean-field theory predicts collapse. The LHY energy, which grows faster with density, halts it. For a balanced mixture with δ=−(g+g12)>0 the energy density is −14δn2+25γmn5/2, and setting the pressure to zero gives a liquid of fixed density (Petrov 2015):

n0=(5δ12γm)2.

The result is a self-bound droplet with a flat top at n0, observed with potassium mixtures (Cabrera et al. 2018; Semeghini et al. 2018). In strictly 2D the LHY term becomes logarithmic (Petrov and Astrakharchik 2016); the app keeps the 3D form, which suits a quasi-2D gas whose axial width is large compared with the scattering lengths.

Try this

  1. The miscibility threshold. Raise g12/g from 0.6 through 1.0 and predict where separation begins. Watch the domain walls sharpen as you go further past 1.

  2. Filled vortex cores. At g12/g=0.9, with “Act on red only” on, tap a vortex into the red species. The teal species flows into the empty core (BBP §11.6).

  3. A quantum droplet. With no trap, set g12/g=−1.3, so δ = 0.3 and the predicted flat-top density is n0≈9.8. Move to −1.5 (n0≈27) and watch the droplet shrink and densify.

Lasers can couple two internal states of an atom so that switching state also kicks its momentum. The atoms behave as if they had spin–orbit coupling, and the condensate can form stripes: a density wave that coexists with superfluidity.

The model

H0=12(px−ksoσz)2+12py2+Ω2σx,

the scheme used at NIST (Lin, Jiménez-García and Spielman 2011), with Raman coupling Ω, recoil momentum kso, and the two-component interactions of the previous model with g11=g22=g and cross-interaction g12.

The physics

The single-particle energies form two bands,

E±(𝐤)=12k2+12kso2±kso2kx2+Ω2/4.

For Ω<2kso2 the lower band has two minima at ±k1, with k1=kso1−(Ω/2kso2)2; above that there is one minimum at k = 0. Interactions choose how the atoms fill the minima (Ho and Zhang 2011; Li, Pitaevskii and Stringari 2012). When g12<g they occupy both, and the interference gives stripes of period π/k1, a state with both superfluid and crystalline order that is a form of supersolid (Li et al. 2017; BBP §13.3). When g12>g they choose one minimum, a plane wave. For large Ω they sit at zero momentum. Interactions shift these boundaries slightly from the single-particle values.

With kso=0 this is a coherently (Rabi) coupled mixture. The coupling locks the relative phase of the two species, so a vortex in one species alone drags a domain wall of relative phase behind it (Son and Stephanov 2002).

Try this

  1. Predict the stripe period. At kso=2, Ω = 4 and g12/g=0.5, predict k1=23/4≈1.73 and a period π/k1≈1.8, about seven stripes across the field. Count them.

  2. Find the transition to zero momentum. Raise Ω and watch the stripes widen as k1 shrinks. Predict where they vanish: Ω=2kso2=8.

  3. Stripe or plane wave. Set g12/g=1.2 at Ω = 4. The stripes give way to a single plane wave, with one species dominating.

  4. A confined vortex. With kso=0 and Ω = 1, tap a vortex into the red species only and watch the relative phase. The phase jump is confined to a line from the vortex: a domain wall.

Replace the atoms' contact interactions with Newtonian gravity and the same equation describes a self-gravitating quantum fluid: a model for ultralight (“fuzzy”) dark matter and boson stars, where quantum pressure resists gravitational collapse.

The model

i∂ψ∂t=[−12∇2+V+g|ψ|2+Φ]ψ,Φ~(𝐤)=−G2πkerfcx(klz2)n~(𝐤),

the Schrödinger–Poisson system with Newtonian gravity averaged over the thin axial profile (erfcxx=ex2erfcx). At long wavelengths this is the potential of a thin sheet, −2πGn~/k. The periodic box removes the uniform background, as cosmological codes do.

The physics

Linearising about a uniform density n gives the Bogoliubov spectrum with gravity included:

ω2=k44+gnk2−2πGnkerfcx(klz2).

Gravity wins at long wavelengths: this is the Jeans instability. Quantum pressure (the k4 term) and contact repulsion stabilise short wavelengths, giving a quantum Jeans length below which structure cannot form (Chavanis 2011; Hui et al. 2017). In an infinite sheet any G is unstable at small enough k. The periodic box only allows k≥2π/L, so the uniform gas is stable until G passes a threshold. After collapse, quantum pressure supports dense solitonic cores, the analogue of the cores found in simulations of fuzzy dark-matter haloes (Schive, Chiueh and Broadhurst 2014). Condensates as models of dark-matter haloes are discussed briefly in BBP §1.3.

Try this

  1. Predict the Jeans threshold. With no trap, the uniform density is n=475/172≈1.64 and the longest wave has k=2π/17≈0.37. Set ω² = 0 in the dispersion relation and solve for G; you should get about 0.09. Then raise G across it and watch the gas fragment.

  2. Cores and their merging. Well above threshold, at G ≈ 0.2, filaments feed dense cores that orbit and merge. Use the phase view to see the interference between cores.

  3. Gravity in a trap. In the harmonic trap, raise G and watch self-gravity compress the cloud beyond the Thomas–Fermi profile.

Exciton-polaritons, part light and part matter, live in semiconductor microcavities. They leak out of the cavity in picoseconds, so a polariton condensate exists only while it is pumped: a superfluid far from equilibrium.

The model

i∂ψ∂t=[−12∇2+V+g|ψ|2+gRnR+i2(RnR−γc)]ψ,∂nR∂t=P−(γR+R|ψ|2)nR,

the driven-dissipative GPE of Wouters and Carusotto (2007). An incoherent reservoir nR, filled by the pump P, feeds the condensate by stimulated scattering at rate R, while polaritons decay at rate γc. Here γc=1, γR=2, R = 0.3 and gR=1. The noise slider adds the truncated-Wigner noise that gain and loss imply (Wouters and Savona 2009), and the disorder slider adds a random potential like the imperfections of a real cavity.

The physics

Gain balances loss when the reservoir is clamped at nR=γc/R, which requires P>Pth=γcγR/R. Above threshold, for a uniform pump, the condensate density is

n=P−Pthγc=γRR(PPth−1),

so it is set by the balance of gain and loss rather than by a conserved atom number (Carusotto and Ciuti 2013). The reservoir also repels the condensate, which therefore flows outwards from a pumped spot. Vortices form spontaneously where such flows meet or where disorder pins them (Lagoudakis et al. 2008; Keeling and Berloff 2008). Polariton condensation was first observed by Kasprzak et al. (2006); BBP mentions polaritons in §1.3 and polariton supersolids in §13.3.

Try this

  1. Threshold. With no trap (uniform pump), lower P/Pth below 1 and the fluid fades; raise it above 1 and a condensate grows from noise. The predicted density is n≈6.7(P/Pth−1), about 10 at the default 2.5.

  2. Outflow from a spot. With the Gaussian spot (the harmonic button), show the phase: the colour cycles outwards from the centre, which is the radial flow driven by reservoir repulsion and gain.

  3. Pinned vortices. Raise the disorder and mark vortices. Do they sit still where they would drift in a clean cavity?

At finite temperature the condensate is surrounded by thermally excited atoms. In two dimensions thermal fluctuations destroy true long-range order, and superfluidity is lost when vortex–antivortex pairs unbind: the Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless (BKT) transition.

The model

dψ=𝒫{−(i+γ)(L−μ)ψdt+dW},L=−12∇2+V+g|ψ|2,⟨dW*dW⟩=2γTdt,

the simple-growth stochastic projected GPE (Gardiner and Davis 2003; Bradley, Gardiner and Davis 2008). The highly occupied low-energy modes, |𝐤|<kc=6.5, are treated as a classical field ψ. They exchange atoms and energy with a thermal reservoir at temperature T and chemical potential μ = 12, through damping γ and noise dW obeying a fluctuation–dissipation relation. The projector 𝒫 removes modes above the cutoff.

The physics

In 2D, long-wavelength phase fluctuations destroy true condensation at any T > 0, but at low T phase correlations decay only as a power law: a quasi-condensate that is still superfluid. Thermally excited vortices appear in bound vortex–antivortex pairs. At TBKT the pairs unbind, free vortices proliferate, and the superfluid density jumps to zero, with nsλT2=4 just below the transition (Berezinskii 1972; Kosterlitz and Thouless 1973). The transition was seen in a 2D atomic gas by Hadzibabic et al. (2006), and classical-field simulations show the pair unbinding directly (Simula and Blakie 2006). Classical-field results depend on the cutoff kc, so the transition temperature in the app is a property of this model rather than a universal number (Blakie et al. 2008). BBP does not cover BKT; its §10.9–10.10 describe 2D vortex gases and how vortices cluster and annihilate.

Try this

  1. Pairs, then free vortices. With no trap, mark vortices and raise T from 4 to 15. Below about 10 there are few vortices, in tight opposite-sign pairs. By 15 they are numerous and unpaired.

  2. Phase coherence. Show the phase at T = 4 and T = 15. At low T the phase varies smoothly over the whole box; above the transition it is scrambled on short scales.

  3. A trapped gas. In the harmonic trap, raise T and watch a thermal cloud grow at the edge as the condensate shrinks. The density shown is averaged over 0.25 time units, as an absorption image would blur it.
